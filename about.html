About Aiwan-e-Jamhoor

Aiwan-e-Jamhoor — ایوانِ جمہور, the People's House — is an open record of every general election to Pakistan's National Assembly since 1977: eleven elections, every directly elected seat, every candidate and every vote, drawn on the constituency boundaries that were actually in force at the time.

Pakistan's election results are public, but they are not very transparent. They live as thousands of separate constituency pages, as scanned Form-47 PDFs, and in unofficial transcriptions of returns the Commission itself has since taken down. The boundaries are worse: the Election Commission has never released a GIS file, so every constituency map in circulation — including this one — is a digitisation of low-resolution paper sheets or a reconstruction from written seat descriptions. This project assembles both halves into one place, and is explicit about where each line and each number came from.

The ambition

The longer aim is bigger than an archive. Wherever elections are taken seriously, a tradition of psephology — the serious, public study of a country's own elections — grows up alongside them, and it is part of how democratic culture reproduces itself: voters can see how they voted, parties can be held to their records, and claims about mandates can be checked against numbers. Pakistan has never really had this. Aiwan-e-Jamhoor wants to help build it: constituency-level analysis as a public habit, honest public polling, swing and turnout treated as things citizens are entitled to understand — a small contribution to a culture and tradition of democratic politics in Pakistan.

Built by

Hiba Sameen is an economist and data scientist based in London. She holds a PhD in Economics and has worked across academia, government and think tanks on economic policy, with an interest in data science and engineering focused on making public data more accessible. Aiwan-e-Jamhoor grew out of the same frustration as its sister project, Data Darbar: the answers are in the public record, but nobody can get at them.

Sources

  • Colin Cookman's open election datasets 1993 · 1997 · 2002 · 2008 · 2013 · 2018
    Candidate- and constituency-level returns derived from Election Commission records and released under GPL-3.0 — pakistan_elections and pakistan_election_results_2018.
  • ElectionPakistani 1977 · 1985 · 1988 · 1990 · 2024
    An unofficial transcription of Commission returns, scraped constituency by constituency and parsed here. It is the only consolidated public series for the pre-1993 elections.
  • Official ECP Form-47 scans 2024
    254 scanned provisional consolidation forms, read directly for registered voters, turnout, polling stations and rejected votes, and arithmetic-checked row by row.
  • rugpundit — PakistanConstituencies2013 2002 delimitation
    The de-facto research standard for the 272-seat 2002 boundaries, itself an unofficial digitisation of Commission raster maps (GPL-3.0).
  • ECP delimitation map sheets 2018 & 2023 delimitations
    114 district sheets for 2018 and the 2023 sheets and published seat compositions, digitised and reconstructed in this project. No official vector file exists for either.
  • geoBoundaries, OCHA COD and GADM district base layers
    The district geometry the pre-2002 reconstructions and the composition-based rebuilds are anchored to.
  • Wikimedia Commons election maps cross-check only
    Independently drawn, seat-numbered result maps by Saad Ali Khan Pakistan (CC BY-SA 4.0), used to verify 1,023 of our winners. Nothing from them is redistributed here.
What this is not Aiwan-e-Jamhoor is an independent civic-data project and is not affiliated with the Election Commission of Pakistan. The boundaries are unofficial reconstructions, the 2024 results are provisional and predate tribunal revisions, and the site shows the election-day picture rather than the current composition of the House. The Method page sets out every limitation in one place; read it before citing anything here.
